A binary mask can only return whole pixels, so its width quantises to the GSD. Sub-pixel estimation reads the partial-area response at the crack edge off the probability map and keeps resolving below one pixel, until the signal itself is gone. Both are shown against the same known truth.

Patch inference is the right call for micro-cracks: a crack three pixels wide disappears when a 8000 px image is resized to fit a network. But tiling splits continuous topology, and length is the metric that splitting destroys. One crack becomes one instance per tile it touches, each reporting its own fragment.

How stitching works

Three rules, run after inference and before measurement:

1. Overlap, don't abut. Tiles overlap by at least twice the maximum expected crack width, so every crack is seen whole in at least one tile.

2. Match at the seam. Two fragments join if their skeleton endpoints land within the overlap band and their tangent directions agree to within about 20 degrees.

3. Re-measure after joining, never add the fragment lengths. A joined skeleton is re-walked end to end, because the sum of fragment lengths loses the part inside the seam and double counts the overlap.

The false-positive list in the brief is the right one. What it does not say is that the hardest true positive and the most common false positive are the same shape in the same place: a dark line inside a mortar joint. A model trained to suppress mortar lines suppresses the findings that matter most.

!
This is the one that decides whether the model is usable on masonry. Cracks follow mortar because mortar is the weak path, so the discriminator cannot be "is it in a joint". It has to be built on continuity across courses, aperture variation along the run, and whether the feature crosses brick as well as joint.
